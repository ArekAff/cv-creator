# The sense-making interview, adapted for CV building

Source: Linderman, A., Baker, J. & Bosacker, S. C. (2011). *Surfacing and transferring expert
knowledge: the sense-making interview.* Human Resource Development International, 14(3), 353–362
(`expert-knowledge/HRDIArticle.pdf`). The authors' "Sagis Interview" adapts Brenda Dervin's
Sense-Making Methodology (SMM). Page numbers below refer to the journal pagination.

This file is the operating protocol for every interview in this project: the first career
interview, the gap interview for a job ad, and the quick debrief of a recent experience. Read it
before interviewing. The data formats are in `method/knowledge-base.md`.

---

## 1. Why this method fits CV writing

- **The easy layer is not where the value is.** Who / what / where / when facts are simple to
  collect (p. 354). The *how*, *when* and *why it worked* is tacit, intuitive and hard to put into
  words. Generic CVs come from the easy layer ("Responsible for reporting"). Strong CVs come from the
  tacit layer: what the person saw, weighed, decided and achieved.
- **People move through time, from action to action** (Fig. 1, p. 355). Actions are "planks" on a
  time line, started by the person or by someone or something else. Between actions there is a
  *gap*, however short, where the person makes sense of the situation: assesses it, asks questions,
  draws conclusions, decides. Most of that sense-making is forgotten as the next action starts.
- **The gap is the doorway.** Neutral questions about the gaps let the person *relive* the
  experience, and in doing so put their own intelligence into words (p. 356). Interviewees often
  say something like "now I understand why I did that" (p. 357).
- **Expertise is how someone bridges gaps** (p. 360): the conditions that hold progress back, the
  questions they ask themselves between key actions, the options they weigh, their wishes and
  preferences, and the ideas, decisions and conclusions they reach. That is the raw material of
  accomplishment statements and of honest evidence for every skill a job ad asks for.

## 2. Interviewer skills: always on

### 2.1 Explicit verbal patterning (p. 356)
1. **Use their nouns, not yours.** Nouns are the most easily misunderstood words. People are the
   theorists of their own world and work best in their own vocabulary. If they say "the Monday
   pack", you say "the Monday pack", not "the weekly KPI reporting". Translating into job-ad
   keywords happens **later**, when drafting the CV, and is recorded in the trail's `keywords`.
2. **Phrase probes with movement, activity and emotion.** Use "what's happening", "where are you
   heading", "what's pulling at you". Avoid static "what was the situation".
3. **Shift to the present tense to take them back.** When you enter a gap, set the scene and switch
   tense: *"It's September 2022. You've just read the email from your boss. What's happening at
   this moment? What are you seeing?"* Present tense gives the most vivid recall.

### 2.2 Artful use of language (p. 356)
- **Never ask "why?".** It invites speculation about motive, defensiveness and justification, and
  pulls the person away from what actually happened. The probe categories below cover the same
  ground without that cost.
- **Do not ask them to interpret** a fact or an experience ("what did that mean?", "was that the
  right call?"). Interpretation brings in hindsight and defence.
- **Do not ask them to look back** ("looking back…", "in hindsight…", "what did you learn?"). Ask
  them to *go back* to the moment instead.

| Instead of… | Ask… |
|---|---|
| Why did you do that? | What were you hoping would happen? / What were you trying to figure out right then? |
| What did you learn from it? | You've just finished X. What's happening now? What are you deciding? |
| Was that a good decision? | What options are you weighing at this point? |
| What was the situation? | It's [date]. You've just [action]. How do you see yourself at this moment? |
| What were your responsibilities? | (scaffold only) Walk me through what you looked after, one item at a time. |

### 2.3 Other tips from the article (pp. 360–361)
- **You do not need to be an expert in their field.** Ask them to explain in their words; use
  logic to notice when intelligence is being used. Never correct their terms.
- **Iterate with the sponsor.** Here the person is both the expert and the sponsor. Report what
  you have learned along the way and let them change focus. What seems important at the start
  often shifts once unknown expertise appears.
- **Define key objectives first.** The authors' only failed project lacked a clear objective. Start
  every session by agreeing what this session is for.
- **Stick to the trails.** People enjoy telling stories. Bring them back, kindly, to the gaps of
  the trail you are on.
- **Repetition is a feature.** The same probes come back gap after gap. It feels redundant at
  first, but it surfaces less conscious knowledge (p. 358). People quickly get into the rhythm.
- **Name recurring themes.** For example: "That's the third time you've mentioned working with
  the warehouse staff. What's the significance of that for you?" (p. 362). Record it in
  `insights.yaml`.

## 3. The three steps (p. 357)

### Step 1: Identify the strategic trails
A *trail* is one exemplary experience: a bounded period in which the person pursued a strategy
towards a result. In the article, the interviewers took the job description item by item, checked
each role and responsibility, ranked them by value, and chose a time period for each high-value
one (p. 357). Nine trails came out of one expert's career (Fig. 2, p. 358). Trails can come from
different, disconnected periods.

For a CV:
- **Career interview (no job ad):** go through each role from the scaffold. List its
  responsibilities item by item, in their words, and let them prioritise (`priority: high | medium
  | low`). For every high-value responsibility, ask for one concrete episode where they did it
  towards a result. Add episodes they are proud of, where something was at stake, that changed how
  others worked, or that they would want a future employer to know. Aim for 1–3 trails per
  significant recent role.
- **Gap interview (job ad):** the job ad is the "job description". Each must-have requirement
  without solid evidence becomes a question: *is there an episode, at work, in studies, in a side
  project or in volunteering, where you did something like this?* If yes, that episode is the trail.

### Step 2: The action-step outline
For each trail, ask the person to walk through what happened, **one action per line**, with
approximate dates (p. 357). Actions can be theirs or someone else's (a client complaint, a boss's
request, a system failing). Leave out actions that do not matter. Add actions at any time during
the exploration. Example format, as in the article:

```
2022-09  Head of Commercial asks why margin questions keep coming by email   (other)
2022-09  Sits in three regional Monday meetings and notes which numbers are used   (self)
2022-10  Builds a one-page Power BI prototype with five KPIs   (self)
...
```

### Step 3: Gap exploration with the probing cycle
Once the outline is reasonably complete, **go back to the first action** and probe the gap after
it, then the next gap, through the whole trail (p. 358). Each cycle starts with the **Situation**
and then moves around the other four categories of the probing cycle (Fig. 3, p. 359), going deeper
whenever an answer hints at more underneath:

```
         QUESTIONS  <------------->  EMOTIONS
   (curiosities, concerns)       (feelings, impulses)
                 \                  /
                    SITUATION
                 (incident, issue)
                 /                  \
         INTENTIONS <------------->  WISHES
      (ideas, decisions)          (hopes, preferences)
```

| Category | Surfaces | Recorded as (`gaps[]`) | Used on the CV for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Situation**: incident, issue | context, scale, stakes, constraints, how they see themselves | `situation` | the context part of a bullet; scope and scale |
| **Questions**: curiosities, concerns | what they were working out; judgement | `questions` | evidence of analysis and problem-solving |
| **Emotions**: feelings, impulses | what mattered, pressure, energy | `emotions` | **never on the CV**; motivation, values, interview preparation |
| **Wishes**: hopes, preferences | goals, standards, what good looks like to them | `wishes` | the aim of a bullet; values; career direction |
| **Intentions**: ideas, decisions | approaches chosen, decisions taken | `intentions` | action verbs and the "how" of a bullet; decision-making |

Then **outcomes**: what happened next, what it led to, how it helped. These become `outcomes[]`
and supply the results in every bullet.

## 4. The CV adaptation (added by this project)

The article stops at surfacing and representing knowledge (p. 358–359: analysis and synthesis,
then representations a successor can learn from). For a CV, the "representations" are the
master resume and accomplishment statements. Three additions make that work.

### 4.1 Fact pass, after the gaps of a trail
Use plain who / what / where / when / how-much questions, which are the easy layer:
- scale (team size, budget, users, volume, number of sites or clients), tools, methods, dates
- results with numbers **only if they are real**: before → after, % change, time saved, money,
  adoption ("6 of 6 regions"), recognition
- where proof lives (performance review, email, dashboard). Note it in `outcomes[].source` and,
  if the person shares a file, keep it in `profile/evidence/`.

Rules: **never suggest or invent a number.** If they don't know, ask for a defensible range
("roughly how many…? what's the lowest you'd be comfortable defending in an interview?"), or keep
it qualitative. Mark `evidence: user_stated` or `documented`. The guide is blunt: lying or
exaggerating might get an interview, but it won't get them through one.

### 4.2 Sponsor check, at the end of every trail
Read back, briefly:
1. the trail in one sentence,
2. 2–3 draft accomplishment statements (action + context + result, numbers only where real),
3. the competencies it shows, each with its evidence.

Let the person correct the wording. Only confirmed statements go into `accomplishments`.
Unresolved points go into `open_questions`.

### 4.3 Synthesis, at the end of a session (Sagis step 2)
Compare the trails (p. 358). Look for patterns in how this person works, recurring themes, strengths
backed by several trails, values (from emotions and wishes), and career direction. Write them to
`insights.yaml` with `seen_in` references. Draft 1–2 profile summaries in their voice
(`summary_drafts`). Run `uv run cv master` to refresh the master resume.

## 5. Running it in a chat

- **One probe per message**, two at most if they are tightly linked. Keep messages short and in the
  person's language. No lectures about the method. Explain it in two sentences at the start.
- **Mirror their words** in every probe. Quote short phrases back.
- **Show where you are** now and then, lightly: "Trail 2 of 3, gap 3 of 6".
- **Let them set the depth:**
  - *quick*: scaffold plus one trail per recent role, situation/intentions/outcomes only on the 2–3
    pivotal gaps;
  - *standard*: the full probe cycle on the pivotal gaps, lighter elsewhere;
  - *deep*: the full cycle on every gap.

  Pivotal gaps are where a decision was made, an obstacle appeared, or the outcome changed.
- **They can answer several probes at once.** Don't re-ask what they already covered.
- **Comfort first.** Emotions are optional; never push. Don't store sensitive personal data (health,
  family, religion, politics) unless they explicitly want it kept, and it never goes on a CV.
- **Save as you go.** Write to the trail file after every trail and append to the session file
  every few exchanges. A chat can end at any moment; the knowledge must survive it.
- **If they slip into "why" or hindsight answers**, that's fine: record it, then gently take
  them back to the moment ("and right then, what were you seeing?").

## 6. Probe bank

Use these as patterns, rephrased with the person's own nouns. Present tense once inside a gap.

**Situation** (always first in a cycle)
- How do you see yourself at this moment? (article, p. 358)
- What is different about your situation now? (article, p. 358)
- What's happening at this moment? What are you seeing? (article, p. 356)
- Where are you, who's around, what's on the table?

**Questions**
- What questions are going through your mind right now?
- What are you trying to figure out?
- What's puzzling you, or worrying you?

**Emotions** (light touch)
- What's going on for you at this point?
- What's pulling at you right now?

**Wishes**
- What are you hoping will happen?
- What would you like to see here? What would "good" look like?
- What would you prefer to avoid?

**Intentions**
- What are you deciding?
- What ideas are coming to you? What options are you weighing?
- What do you do next?

**Outcomes / helps** (closing a gap)
- What happens as a result?
- How will that help? (article, p. 356)
- What does that lead to?

**Scaffold and fact pass** (plain questions, any tense)
- Walk me through what you looked after in that role, one item at a time.
- Which of those mattered most, to you or to the organisation?
- How many…, how big…, how often…, over what period…? What changed, from what to what?
- Where could someone see the proof of that?

## 7. Starting from an existing CV

When the person brings a CV (skill `import-resume`), the diff of that CV against the knowledge base
becomes the interview agenda. The CV is evidence of what they chose to say, not of what happened.

- **Facts** (employer, title, dates, school, certificate) are the easy who / what / where / when
  layer (p. 354). Confirm them plainly and record them. When the CV and the knowledge base disagree,
  ask which is right now; an old CV is often just older.
- **A bullet is the end of a trail.** It states an outcome and hides the actions and gaps that led
  to it. Turn it into a trail and walk back: "Your CV says '…'. Take me back to when that started.
  What's happening?" Then build the action outline from there and probe the pivotal gaps as usual.
  The person often finds a stronger, truer statement than the CV had, and that's the point.
- **Numbers in a CV are claims.** In the fact pass, ask how they knew the figure, what it was
  measured against, and what they would defend in an interview. Record `evidence: user_stated` or
  `documented` as for any other outcome.
- **Use their nouns, not the CV's.** CV wording is often polished for recruiters. During probing,
  use the words the person uses when they talk about the work.
- **Skills listed without a story** need one anchor: where they used it, roughly when, and how much.
  Otherwise they stay out of the knowledge base, or are recorded with an honest note.
- **New roles** get the full career-interview treatment: responsibilities item by item, priorities,
  then trails for the high-value ones (section 3).
